REIT vs InvIT: What Is the Difference, and Which Fits Your Income Goal?

Both are listed trusts that pay out most of their cash, but they own very different assets and carry different risks. Here is how REITs and InvITs compare on assets, debt, yield, return of capital and tax.

📊 Direct Answer

A REIT owns office parks and malls; an InvIT owns infrastructure such as toll roads, transmission lines and telecom towers. Both must distribute at least 90% of distributable cash flow. REITs can borrow up to 49% of asset value, while InvITs can go up to 70% under conditions. InvITs often quote higher yields, but their assets can have a finite life, so part of each payout can be your own capital returned.

The Short Answer

A REIT owns office parks and malls; an InvIT owns infrastructure such as toll roads, transmission lines and telecom towers. Both are trusts listed on the exchange, both must pay out at least 90% of distributable cash flow, and both pass income through to you. The differences that matter to an investor are how long the assets last, how much debt the trust can carry and what part of each payout is really your own capital coming back.

InvITs often show higher headline yields than REITs. That is partly compensation for risk and partly a feature of assets with a finite life, so the yield on its own is not the return. If you are new to REITs, start with What is a REIT?.

What They Own, and Why It Matters

A REIT's tenants sign leases that usually include rent escalations every few years, and the buildings can be re-let or revalued over decades. Its fortunes follow office and retail demand; see our Q1 FY27 roundup for how that looked this quarter.

An InvIT's cash comes from contracted or regulated infrastructure. A transmission InvIT earns regulated charges that are largely independent of the economy; a toll-road InvIT earns from traffic, which depends on economic activity, fuel prices and alternative routes. SEBI requires public InvITs to hold at least 80% of assets in completed, revenue-generating projects, with up to 10% in projects under construction.

The Part Most Comparisons Skip: Finite Life and Return of Capital

A high InvIT yield is not the same as a high return

Road concessions end, and as they approach the end, distributions increasingly include repayment of your invested capital. Units carry no maturity value. One broker-side explainer notes that people who bought IRB InvIT at its 2017 IPO price could face a loss on sale despite years of payouts. A higher yield, the same source says, reflects higher cash-flow uncertainty, not simply higher value.

Part of any REIT or InvIT payout can be a return of capital, which is not taxed when received but reduces your cost of acquisition for capital gains, as the Income-tax Act's residual-distribution rules work. Keep a record of those amounts each quarter. For an InvIT, expect that share to grow as a concession ages.

Two cautions apply. First, yield equals the latest annualised distribution divided by today's price, so it rises when the price falls, including when the market doubts the payout will last. Second, an InvIT yielding 10% that returns capital is not delivering 10% a year of profit. Compare a REIT's 5-6% yield, which is mostly income and can grow with rent escalations, with that in mind.

Tax: Mostly the Same Mechanics

Both are pass-through trusts. A distribution is taxed by component: interest at your slab rate (with TDS of 10% for residents and 5% for non-residents), rental income at your rate, dividend exempt in your hands following the 2026 amendment, and the residual, such as debt repayment, reduces your cost basis until it reaches zero and is then taxed. Listed units held for more than 12 months pay 12.5% long-term capital gains tax, and shorter holdings pay 20%. From FY 2026-27 an annual exemption threshold of the kind that applies to listed equity may also apply to listed REIT and InvIT units, which our taxation guide explains. See our REIT taxation guide for the component-by-component walk-through; the same logic applies to InvITs, but check each trust's distribution statement because the mix differs.

One structural difference is classification for mutual funds: since 1 January 2026, SEBI treats REITs as equity-related instruments while InvITs stay hybrid, which affects how equity and hybrid funds can hold them.

Risks Side by Side

  • Interest rates. Both trade like yield instruments, and unit prices tend to fall when rates rise.
  • Concentration. REITs depend on office and retail demand in a few cities and on large tenants and GCCs. InvITs depend on a handful of assets, sponsors and counterparties.
  • Asset life and renewal. Road concessions expire. Buildings need re-leasing, and a vacant floor stops paying rent.
  • Regulation and tariffs. Toll rates, transmission tariffs and concession terms can change, and so can tax and SEBI rules.
  • Leverage and refinancing. Higher debt makes distributions more sensitive to the cost of refinancing.
  • Liquidity. Smaller trusts can trade thinly. The 23 listed InvITs range from ₹52,431 crore in market value (Altius Telecom Infrastructure Trust) to ₹2,284 crore (Anantam Highways Trust).

Which Fits Which Goal

A way to think about it, not a recommendation for any unit.

  • You want income that can grow and assets that last. REITs, with rent escalations and re-lettable buildings, fit better, at a lower headline yield.
  • You want a higher cash yield and accept returning capital. InvITs fit, provided you check how much of each payout is capital and how long the concession runs.
  • You want to avoid economic cyclicality. A regulated transmission InvIT is the least tied to traffic or office demand.
  • You want sector diversification. Holding both can spread your exposure across property and infrastructure; see our allocation guide for sizing.

Questions to Ask Before You Buy

  • What share of the last four distributions was interest, dividend, rent and return of capital?
  • For an InvIT, when do the concessions or contracts expire, and what happens afterwards?
  • What are net debt to asset value, the interest cover and the credit rating?
  • Who is the sponsor, and how has the trust treated unitholders on acquisitions and fees?
  • What is the yield on today's price, not the price from the article you read?

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main difference between a REIT and an InvIT?

A REIT owns income-producing real estate such as office parks and malls, while an InvIT owns infrastructure such as toll roads, transmission lines and telecom towers. Both are listed trusts that must distribute at least 90% of distributable cash flow. InvITs often have finite asset lives, so more of each payout can be a return of capital over time.

Is an InvIT riskier than a REIT?

Not automatically, because the risks differ. InvITs often show higher yields, but they can depend on traffic or a few assets, and part of the payout can be your own capital. REITs depend on office and retail demand and tenant leasing. Leverage varies widely within both groups, so read each trust's own figures.

How many InvITs are listed in India?

Screener data reported by Sector Alpha on 11 October 2026 showed 23 listed InvITs with a combined market value of about ₹2.47 lakh crore. A separate analysis by Vinod Kothari counted about 27 SEBI-registered InvITs, including private ones, with assets of about ₹7 lakh crore in November 2025. India has six listed REITs.

Are REIT and InvIT distributions taxed the same way?

The mechanics are the same: interest, dividend, rental income and residual distributions such as debt repayment are treated separately, and listed units pay 12.5% long-term capital gains tax after 12 months and 20% short-term tax. The mix of components differs by trust, so check the distribution statement. Confirm your own position with a chartered accountant.

People Also Ask

Can I buy an InvIT with a small amount?
Yes, for listed public InvITs. They trade on NSE and BSE and you can buy one unit at the market price through a demat account. Private InvITs are different: SEBI cut their minimum allotment to ₹25 lakh in September 2025, so they are not for small investors.
Is an InvIT return of capital taxable?
Not when you receive it. The amount reduces your cost of acquisition, which raises your capital gain when you sell, and once the cost reaches zero further residual distributions are taxed straight away, according to Mondaq's reading of the Income-tax Act, 2025.
What is the borrowing limit for InvITs?
Under regulation 20, as Vinod Kothari describes it: up to 25% of assets needs no extra steps, above 25% and up to 49% needs a credit rating and approval from 50% of unitholders, and above 49% up to 70% needs an AAA rating, six distributions of track record and 75% approval, with funds used only for infrastructure acquisition or development. REITs are capped at 49%.
