GIFT City for NRIs vs Resident Indians: Who Can Invest in What, and How It Is Taxed

The same fund can be tax-free in India for an NRI and fully taxable for a resident. Here is how GIFT City differs for each group: how money gets in, what you can buy, how it is taxed in India and at home, and what changes if you return.

📊 Direct Answer

NRIs wire money to GIFT City from abroad without LRS, can buy inbound funds and IBU deposits, and pay no Indian tax on gains from eligible IFSC funds or on deposit interest, though their home country may tax it. Resident Indians use LRS (USD 250,000 a year, 20% TCS above ₹10 lakh), mainly invest in outbound funds, get no exemption and must disclose holdings in Schedule FA. IBU deposits are not covered by DICGC insurance.

The Short Answer

GIFT City is built differently for the two groups. For an NRI or OCI, it works like an offshore centre that happens to sit in India: money comes in by wire, products are in dollars, Indian tax on eligible fund gains and deposit interest is nil, and getting money out is simple. For a resident Indian, it is a way to invest abroad through the LRS route, with the same USD 250,000 limit, TCS and Schedule FA as any overseas investment, and no tax exemption. The exemptions people quote are for non-residents.

Two more points decide whether it is a good idea. For an NRI, what your country of residence does with the income matters more than what India does. And the benefits depend on residential status at the time you invest, earn and sell, so moving back to India changes the picture. If you are a resident, start with GIFT City vs investing in US stocks directly.

Why Residential Status Decides Everything

Indian tax law and FEMA both split investors by status. An NRI pays Indian tax only on Indian-source income. A resident and ordinarily resident (ROR) is taxed on global income and must disclose foreign assets. An RNOR (resident but not ordinarily resident) sits in between, typically for a limited period after returning to India: foreign income is largely outside Indian tax, and the IBU interest exemption continues. The GIFT City gains exemption is conditional on being a non-resident when you transfer the units, so the same fund can be tax-free for you as an NRI and taxable after you return. Status is tested each year; confirm yours with a chartered accountant before acting.

How the Money Gets In

NRIs. Entities in GIFT City are treated as persons resident outside India for FEMA purposes, so an NRI sends money from an overseas bank account directly, and RBI's LRS does not apply. You open an account with an IFSC Banking Unit (IBU) of a bank such as SBI, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank or Axis Bank, or with a fund platform. Banks differ on funding: some IBUs accept transfers from an NRE or FCNR account and others only wires from abroad, so ask before you open. IFSCA has been rolling out video KYC for NRIs in a set of countries since late 2025.

Residents. You remit through LRS, which caps all your overseas spending, including education, travel and other investments, at USD 250,000 per financial year, and your bank collects 20% TCS on the part above ₹10 lakh for purposes other than education and medical. What Each Group Can Buy

  • Inbound funds take foreign money and invest in Indian securities. Retail examples reported by explainers include the Tata India Dynamic Equity Fund (a feeder into Tata's Indian funds and ETFs, with a USD 500 minimum). These are generally meant for non-residents, and a resident should expect to be turned away from a fund that targets Indian securities.
  • Outbound funds take Indian money and invest abroad, for example the DSP Global Equity Fund (reported minimum USD 5,000). They are the route for residents through LRS.
  • USD deposits at IBUs start at around USD 1,000. As reported, SBI's IBU deposit rates from 17 September 2026 were 3.60% for 7 days to a month, 4.85% for 9 to 12 months, 4.15% for one to three years and 3.65% for three to five years, with ICICI and HDFC quoting a little lower. Rates change, so check the bank's current sheet.
  • AIFs and PMS have higher minimums. Under IFSCA's Fund Management Regulations, investors in a restricted scheme need at least USD 150,000 (USD 40,000 for employees and directors of the fund manager), with accredited investors exempt, and portfolio management starts at USD 75,000. Lock-ups of three to seven years are common for AIFs.

Eligibility is fund-specific. Read the offer document, because some funds exclude investors in certain countries, particularly the US and Canada, because of FATCA compliance. The full list of what exists is in our GIFT City funds and platforms guide.

Indian Tax: Where NRIs Get the Benefit

For a non-resident, gains on units of eligible IFSC funds are exempt in India and carry no TDS, and interest on IBU deposits is exempt with no TDS. Compare that with a mainland Indian equity fund, where an NRI's gains are taxed at 12.5% long-term (above ₹1.25 lakh) or 20% short-term, deducted at source. The exemption was in Section 10(4D) of the Income-tax Act, 1961; the new Income-tax Act, 2025, effective 1 April 2026, gathers IFSC exemptions into its Schedule VI, as NRI Information reports. Check the exact provision with your adviser. The exemption also does not apply to every product: it covers qualifying funds, so an AIF or PMS can be taxed differently depending on how it is structured.

For a resident, there is no equivalent exemption. Gains are taxable by structure, TCS is claimable as credit, and holdings go into Schedule FA; see our GIFT City tax and Schedule FA guide.

The Tax That Often Decides It: Your Country of Residence

India taxing nothing does not mean no one taxes it. As explainers describe the position, and subject to checking with a tax adviser in your country:

  • UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman: no personal income tax, so the benefit is real. Some guides suggest getting a tax residency certificate early.
  • United Kingdom: the full UK rate applies. India withholds nothing, so there is no foreign tax credit to offset it, and offshore funds without reporting status can turn gains into income taxed at higher rates.
  • Canada: worldwide income is taxed, and a credit for Indian tax is of little help when India charged none.
  • United States: IBU interest is taxable with no credit. Pooled GIFT City funds and AIFs are likely to be treated as passive foreign investment companies (PFICs), which brings Form 8621 filings and harsh default tax treatment, and the accounts are reportable under FBAR (above USD 10,000) and FATCA. Some sources suggest portfolio management structures can be simpler than pooled funds, but a US CPA should confirm this.

The India-treaty route does not rescue this, since there is no Indian tax for a treaty to relieve. If you live in the US, UK or Canada, speak to an adviser in that country before sending money.

Getting the Money Out, and What Is Not Insured

IBU accounts keep money in dollars. As explainers describe it, withdrawals are ordinary international wires with no annual ceiling and without Forms 15CA/15CB, and there is no rupee round trip. Money held in a mainland NRO account, by contrast, can be repatriated up to USD 1 million per financial year with Forms 15CA/15CB and a CA certificate, and the rupee conversion at entry and exit adds currency drag over long periods. Ask your bank to confirm the process for your specific product.

The trade-off is protection. IBU deposits are not covered by DICGC deposit insurance, unlike NRE and NRO deposits (up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank), and the regulator is IFSCA rather than RBI or SEBI day to day.

If Your Status Changes

If you return to India, your GIFT City accounts do not have to be closed, but new investments fall under LRS and your global income becomes taxable once you are resident. RNOR status may soften the first years. The exemption on fund gains depends on being non-resident at the time of transfer, so plan the timing of redemptions with a chartered accountant before you move.

Mistakes to Avoid

  • Assuming tax-free everywhere. The exemption is Indian. Your country of residence may tax the full amount.
  • Using a GIFT City account for Indian-source money. Explainers warn that routing India-sourced income such as Indian rent through an IFSC account can breach FEMA, with penalties of up to three times the amount involved.
  • Investing as a resident in a non-resident product. Check eligibility in the offer document, not the marketing page.
  • Ignoring concentration. Most GIFT City funds launched between 2022 and 2025 and the menu is short.
  • Forgetting costs. A feeder fund's fee sits on top of the underlying fund's.

Which Fits Which Situation

This is a framework for thinking about the choice, not a recommendation for any product.

  • An NRI in the Gulf who wants Indian equity exposure in dollars. The inbound-fund and IBU-deposit route can remove Indian tax and the rupee conversion. Compare costs against an NRE route.
  • An NRI in the US, UK or Canada. The home-country tax and reporting position comes first, and eligibility may be restricted. Take advice before you invest.
  • A resident who wants global diversification. GIFT City outbound funds are one route among several and carry no tax benefit. Compare with direct investing and Indian international funds.
  • Someone planning to return to India. Map your timeline before investing, since residency changes the tax result.

Questions to Ask Before You Invest

  • What is my residential status in India today, and what will it be when I redeem?
  • How will my country of residence tax this income, and what reporting does it need?
  • Does the fund accept investors from my country, and does it qualify for the Indian exemption?
  • What are the total costs, including any feeder-fund layer?
  • How exactly will I take money out, and what will the bank need?

Frequently Asked Questions

Can NRIs invest in GIFT City?

Yes. NRIs and OCIs are the main eligible group for inbound funds that invest in Indian securities, for USD deposits at IFSC Banking Units and for AIFs and PMS, subject to each product's offer document. NRIs wire money from an overseas bank account and do not use LRS. Some funds exclude investors in certain countries such as the US and Canada.

Is GIFT City tax-free for NRIs?

Not entirely. Gains on units of eligible IFSC funds and interest on IBU deposits are exempt from Indian tax for a non-resident, but your country of residence may still tax the income, and the exemption depends on the product's structure. It was Section 10(4D) in the 1961 Act, and the Income-tax Act 2025 now carries IFSC exemptions in Schedule VI. Check the exact position with an adviser.

Can a resident Indian invest in GIFT City?

Yes, through the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, which allows up to USD 250,000 per financial year across all purposes, with 20% TCS above ₹10 lakh for purposes other than education and medical. Residents mainly use outbound funds that invest abroad. There is no tax exemption for residents, and holdings must be reported in Schedule FA.

What happens to my GIFT City investment if I return to India?

The accounts need not be closed, but new investments fall under LRS and your global income becomes taxable once you are resident, with RNOR status possibly easing the first years. The gains exemption depends on being non-resident when you transfer the units, so time any redemptions with a chartered accountant before you move.

People Also Ask

Do NRIs need a PAN to invest in GIFT City?
It depends on the product. Some retail fund investments eligible for the Indian exemption may not require PAN, while many other transactions and most AIFs do, so check the offer document and platform requirements.
Are GIFT City deposits safer than NRE deposits?
They are insured differently. IBU deposits are not covered by DICGC, while NRE and NRO deposits are covered up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank. IFSCA regulates the zone, not RBI day to day.
Do US-based NRIs face extra rules on GIFT City funds?
Often yes. Explainers say pooled funds and AIFs are likely to be treated as PFICs for US tax, which means Form 8621 filings, and GIFT City accounts appear on FBAR and FATCA reports. Some funds also exclude US investors. Take advice from a US CPA before investing.
