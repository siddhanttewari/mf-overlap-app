How Much Should You Allocate to REITs? A Framework

REITs are easy to buy — one click, one unit, no minimum ticket to speak of. That ease makes it tempting to skip the harder question: how much is actually the right amount for your portfolio?

📊 Key Point

A commonly cited range among advisors is 5-15% of a diversified portfolio for REITs, treated as a satellite allocation rather than a core holding. The right number for you depends on existing real estate exposure (including your own home), income needs, and how much interest-rate sensitivity you're comfortable adding to your portfolio.

Why Sizing Matters More Than Picking

Most REIT content focuses on which REIT to buy — occupancy rates, sponsor quality, yield comparisons. But for most investors, how much to allocate matters more to overall portfolio outcomes than which specific REIT to pick, especially since India's REITs are reasonably correlated with each other (they're all exposed to similar interest rate and commercial real estate cycles, even across office vs retail). Getting the allocation size right is the higher-leverage decision.

Where the 5-15% Range Comes From

A commonly cited guideline among advisors suggests keeping REIT exposure to roughly 5-15% of a diversified portfolio, treating it as a satellite allocation for income and diversification rather than a core building block like equity or debt. This isn't a regulatory rule — it's a rough industry convention based on REITs being a single asset class with its own risk factors (interest rate sensitivity, sector concentration) that shouldn't dominate a portfolio the way a diversified equity or debt allocation can. Where you land within that range depends on the factors below.

Account for Existing Real Estate Exposure

Before sizing a REIT allocation, take stock of real estate you already have exposure to. Most advisors suggest treating your primary residence separately from your investment portfolio sizing — it serves shelter, not liquid investment return, and isn't something you'd typically rebalance. But if you own investment property beyond your primary home, or hold real estate/sectoral mutual funds, that exposure should factor into how much additional REIT allocation makes sense — piling a large REIT position on top of an already real-estate-heavy portfolio concentrates risk rather than diversifying it.

Income Needs vs Growth Needs

REITs are structurally built for income — the SEBI-mandated 90% distribution requirement means regular payouts are a core feature, not incidental. If you're specifically seeking regular income (common for retirees or anyone building a passive income stream), a REIT allocation on the higher end of the range may make sense. If you're earlier in a wealth-building phase and prioritizing growth, a smaller allocation — treating REITs purely as a diversifier rather than an income engine — may fit better, since REIT total returns have historically shown more modest capital appreciation than growth-oriented equity.

Interest Rate Sensitivity

REIT unit prices tend to move inversely with interest rates — rising rates can pressure valuations since fixed income becomes more competitive on yield, and REITs often carry meaningful debt at the SPV level that becomes more expensive to service. If your portfolio is already carrying significant interest-rate-sensitive exposure (long-duration bonds, for instance), a large REIT allocation compounds that sensitivity rather than diversifying against it. Factor in your total portfolio's rate sensitivity, not just the REIT allocation in isolation.

One REIT or Several?

Within your REIT allocation, consider whether to concentrate in one REIT or spread across a few. Since Nexus Select Trust is the only listed retail REIT, an all-office-REIT allocation (Embassy, Mindspace, Brookfield, Knowledge Realty Trust) carries pure office-sector risk, while adding Nexus introduces genuine sector diversification within the REIT sleeve itself — see our REIT comparison for how the five differ. For a REIT allocation on the smaller end (5% or less of total portfolio), one or two REITs may be practical; a larger allocation makes a case for spreading across more.

A Simple Sizing Framework

Putting it together, a few questions worth answering before settling on a number: Do I already have significant real estate exposure beyond my primary home? If yes, lean toward the lower end of 5-15% or skip additional REIT exposure for now. Am I primarily seeking income or growth? Income-focused investors can lean higher within the range; growth-focused investors can lean lower. How much interest-rate sensitivity does my existing portfolio already carry? A rate-sensitive portfolio argues for a smaller REIT addition. Do I want diversification within my REIT sleeve itself? If so, plan to spread across at least an office REIT and Nexus for retail exposure rather than concentrating in one. There's no single right answer, but working through these explicitly beats picking a round number because it sounds reasonable.

Frequently Asked Questions

What percentage of my portfolio should be in REITs?

There's no universal number, but a commonly cited range among advisors is roughly 5-15% of a diversified portfolio, treating REITs as a satellite allocation rather than a core holding. The right figure depends on your existing real estate exposure (including a home you own), income needs, and overall risk tolerance.

Should I count my own home when sizing a REIT allocation?

Many advisors suggest treating your primary residence separately from your investment portfolio, since it serves a different purpose (shelter, not liquid investment return) and isn't easily rebalanced. However, if you also own investment property beyond your primary home, that real estate exposure is worth factoring in before adding a REIT allocation, to avoid unintentionally over-concentrating in real estate as an asset class.

Can REITs replace bonds in a portfolio?

Not directly — REITs and bonds have different risk profiles. Bonds offer fixed coupons and principal return at maturity; REITs offer variable distributions and no capital guarantee, with returns that can be more volatile than bonds despite a similar-looking yield. REITs are better thought of as a distinct asset class alongside equity, debt, and other alternatives, not a bond substitute.

People Also Ask

Should I invest in REITs via SIP or lumpsum?
Since REIT units trade like stocks, there's no built-in SIP mechanism the way mutual funds have, but you can manually buy units periodically to average your entry price over time, similar to a self-managed SIP into any listed security.
Does REIT allocation change as I get closer to retirement?
Many investors increase income-generating allocations like REITs as they shift from wealth accumulation to income generation closer to or during retirement, though the right glide path depends on your total portfolio and specific income needs.
How often should I rebalance a REIT allocation?
There's no fixed rule, but reviewing alongside your broader portfolio rebalancing schedule (commonly annual or semi-annual) is a reasonable default, adjusting if REIT price movements have pushed the allocation meaningfully away from your target percentage.
