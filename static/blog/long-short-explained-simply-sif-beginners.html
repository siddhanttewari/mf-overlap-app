Long and Short Explained Simply: How Long-Short Funds Work (A Beginner's Guide to SIFs)

"Long" and "short" sound like jargon, but each is a simple bet. Here is how they work, why a fund would do both at once, and what can go wrong, explained so a child could follow.

📊 Direct Answer

Long means you buy something and hope its price rises. Short means you bet its price will fall: you sell something you borrowed or hold through a contract, then buy it back later at a lower price. A long-short fund makes both bets to soften the ups and downs. It usually gives up some gains in a rising market in return for smaller falls, and it can do worse than a plain fund if the manager is wrong on both sides. India's SIFs bring this into the mutual fund framework, from ₹10 lakh.

🧒 Like you are 5

A long is a bet that something gets more valuable. A short is a bet that it gets less valuable. A long-short fund makes both kinds of bets at once, like walking with one foot on the gas and one hovering over the brake.

What does "long" mean?

"Going long" is just buying something and holding it. If you buy a share, a gold coin or a house, you are long. You make money when the price rises and lose money when it falls. Most mutual funds you own are long-only: they buy shares and hope they go up. (Arbitrage funds also hold hedges, but they are not betting on a fall.)

The most you can lose being long is what you paid (if you did not borrow). The share can fall to zero, but not below.

What does "short" mean?

"Going short" is the opposite bet: you profit when the price falls. Here is the idea with something a child would understand.

The shiny-card story

  1. Your friend has a shiny card that sells for ₹100. You think the price will drop.
  2. You borrow the card and sell it today for ₹100. You now hold ₹100 cash and you owe your friend one card.
  3. Next week the price falls to ₹70. You buy an identical card for ₹70 and give it back.
  4. You kept ₹30. That is a successful short.
  5. But if the price had jumped to ₹140, you would pay ₹140 to give the card back and lose ₹40.

LONG

Buy first, sell later

Buy ₹100→Wait→Sell later

You win if the price goes up (sell at ₹130, gain ₹30). You lose if it goes down.

SHORT

Sell first, buy back later

Borrow & sell ₹100→Wait→Buy back, return it

You win if the price goes down (buy back at ₹70, keep ₹30). You lose if it goes up.

Why shorting is riskier than it sounds

A price cannot fall below zero, so the most a short can gain is 100%. But a price can rise without any limit, so a short can lose much more than it started with. That is why SEBI caps how much an Indian SIF can short. As an investor you hold fund units, which cannot fall below zero, so your own loss is capped at what you put in. The fund itself can still lose a lot if its short bets go wrong.

Why would a fund do both?

Think of a boat in a storm. Being long is the sail: it catches the wind when the market is rising. Being short is the anchor: it holds you back when the market is falling. A long-short manager tries to carry a smaller sail and a little anchor so the boat rocks less. Here is the same idea with money. Imagine a fund with ₹100. It puts ₹70 in shares it likes (long) and uses a contract to bet ₹30 against the market (short). Compare it with a fund that only owns the ₹70 of shares:

The words you will hear

WordPlain meaning
LongYou own it. You hope it goes up.
ShortYou bet it goes down. You borrow or use a contract, sell now, and buy back later.
HedgeA bet placed to cancel part of another risk, like an umbrella. Rain may not come, but you are not soaked if it does.
Derivative (futures, options)A contract whose price follows something else, like a share or the Nifty. This is how Indian funds take short positions.
Net exposureLong minus short. A fund with 70% long and 30% short has 40% net exposure and behaves roughly like a 40% equity fund.
Gross exposureLong plus short. The same fund has 100% gross exposure: it has that much money at work in total.
Unhedged shortA short that is not cancelled by something you own. This is a real bet on a fall, so it can lose money.
Basis riskThe hedge does not move in step with what it protects, so you are only partly covered.
DrawdownHow far a fund falls from its high before it recovers.

A real example of net exposure: INDmoney reported that a Quant factsheet for July 2026 showed average long exposure of about 41%, short about 8% and net about 33% (it does not say which scheme). Long 41 minus short 8 leaves 33, so that fund behaved like roughly a one-third-equity fund at the time, whatever its name says. This is why we say: read the exposure, not just the label.

How an Indian SIF does this

Ordinary mutual funds in India cannot make a stand-alone bet against the market; they use derivatives only to hedge or to run arbitrage. A Specialised Investment Fund (SIF) is allowed to, within limits, using derivatives. You need at least ₹10 lakh to invest. Our SIF guide has the full rules. What can go wrong

  1. The manager is wrong. Own the losers and short the winners and you lose on both sides (row D above).
  2. The hedge slips. If the protection does not move in step with what it protects (basis risk), you are only partly covered.
  3. You give up gains. In a strong rally, the short part is a drag. Globally, long-short funds have usually lagged long-only funds in big bull markets.
  4. It is hard to see inside. A plain equity fund shows a stock list. A long-short fund also has contracts, and the label does not tell you how much is net long.
  5. Short history. Indian SIFs are under a year old. A good few months is not the same as a proven method.
  6. You cannot always sell today. Many SIFs let you redeem only once or twice a week, and a notice period is possible.

Can you say these in one sentence?

Before putting ₹10 lakh anywhere, try to finish these sentences without looking anything up. If you cannot, the fund is not ready for you, and that is fine.

  • This fund makes money when ______ happens, and loses when ______ happens.
  • Its long part is ______ and its short part is ______.
  • Its net exposure to shares is about ______%.
  • The way it gets hurt is ______.
  • I can get my money out ______ (daily, weekly, with notice).
  • This money is about ______% of what I can invest, and I will not need it for ______ years.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does long and short mean in investing?

Long means you own something and profit if its price rises. Short means you bet its price will fall: you borrow or use a contract, sell now, and buy it back later, profiting if it got cheaper and losing if it got dearer.

What is a long-short fund?

A fund that holds some investments it expects to rise (long) and uses derivatives to bet against others or against the market (short). The aim is usually a smoother ride than a long-only fund, not necessarily higher returns.

Can I lose more than I invest in a long-short SIF?

No. A fund unit cannot fall below zero, so an investor's loss is capped at the amount invested. But you can lose a large part of it, and the fund's own short bets can lose more than they started with if the price rises sharply.

Why can mutual funds not go short but SIFs can?

SEBI's rules for ordinary mutual funds do not allow stand-alone short bets (derivatives are for hedging and arbitrage). SIFs were created in 2025 as a middle tier between mutual funds and PMS or AIFs, with a ₹10 lakh minimum, and may take short positions through derivatives within SEBI's limits.

Is a long-short fund safer than an equity fund?

Not automatically. The short part can reduce losses in a falling market, but it can also lose money when the market rises or when the manager picks badly. Check the fund's net equity exposure and how it behaved in past falls instead of relying on the name.

How do I know how much a SIF is long and short?

Look in the fund house's monthly portfolio disclosure and factsheet for long, short and net exposure, and read the scheme information document for the allowed ranges. Ask before investing if it is not shown.

People Also Ask

What is the difference between hedging and shorting?
Shorting is the act of betting on a fall. Hedging is the purpose: using a short (or another contract) to cancel part of the risk of something you own. A short with nothing to protect is a pure bet; a short against something you own is a hedge.
Is short selling legal in India?
Yes, within SEBI's rules. Retail investors can short through derivatives, and regulated funds such as SIFs may take short positions through derivatives within limits. Ordinary mutual funds may not take stand-alone short bets.
Do long-short funds always beat the market?
No. Globally they have usually trailed the market in strong rallies and lost less in major falls. See our guide to how long-short strategies have performed globally.
